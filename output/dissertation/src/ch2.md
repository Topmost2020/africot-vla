# Chapter 2: Literature Review

This chapter reviews the work on which the proposed traffic-control system builds. It begins with traffic signal control, from fixed-time plans through to control by large language models, then turns to latent world models and planning, and to vision-language-action models and chain-of-thought reasoning for control. It then considers explainability and the regulatory setting, and the infrastructure constraints of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The chapter closes with a synthesis that states the gap the dissertation addresses. Where the literature does not support a claim, the claim is omitted or is stated as the author's own reasoning.

## 2.1 Traffic Signal Control

### 2.1.1 Fixed-time and actuated control

The simplest form of signal control repeats a pre-computed cycle whose phase durations are set from historical demand. Fixed-time plans need no sensing and little computation, which helps to explain their persistence in cities with limited detector coverage. Their weakness is that the plan cannot respond to demand that departs from the assumptions used to set it, such as a market day, a stalled vehicle or a burst of motorcycle traffic. Actuated control relaxes this by extending or terminating a phase according to detector calls, so that green time follows the presence of vehicles at the stop line within bounds set by the engineer. Surveys of the field treat these two families as the conventional baseline against which adaptive and learning-based methods are compared (Wei et al., 2019c). Both families are rule-based and local to one junction, and neither is designed to carry a written justification of why a particular phase was chosen. Both nevertheless have a property that matters for this dissertation: they continue to operate when communication fails, because nothing outside the junction is required. The proposed system retains a fixed-time plan for exactly this reason, as the last tier of its degradation ladder.

### 2.1.2 Max-pressure and queue-based adaptive control

Max-pressure control is a decentralised policy for a network of signalised intersections that chooses, at each decision point, the phase that relieves the greatest pressure, where pressure is the difference between the queue upstream of a movement and the queues on the downstream links it feeds (Varaiya, 2013). The method needs only queue-length information local to each junction and its neighbours, requires no knowledge of demand, and is accompanied by an analytical stability result under the model assumptions of the original work. Because it is inexpensive, interpretable in its own terms and well understood, it has become a standard non-learning adaptive baseline in the traffic literature, and it is the reference against which the controllers in this dissertation are compared. Its limits are also visible from its definition: it optimises a queue-difference quantity rather than travel time or the priority of particular road users, and it has no mechanism for taking into account context such as a market, an event or an incident. These are the gaps that a slower, context-reading layer is intended to fill.

The relevance of max-pressure extends beyond its use as a baseline. Wei et al. (2019a) used the pressure concept to design the state and reward of a reinforcement-learning agent, which links the analytic and learned traditions. This connection is one reason for expressing the interface between the slow and fast loops, as described in the methodology chapter, as weights on queue-related terms rather than as direct phase commands.

### 2.1.3 Reinforcement-learning methods

Reinforcement learning (RL) treats signal control as a sequential decision problem in which an agent observes a traffic state, selects a phase or a phase duration, and receives a reward derived from queues, delay or throughput. Wei et al. (2019c) survey this literature and organise it by state representation, action definition and reward design. For arterials and networks, CoLight introduced graph attention so that an intersection can weigh the influence of its neighbours when choosing an action (Wei et al., 2019b), and Chu et al. (2020) developed a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning scheme intended for large-scale networks. PressLight, noted above, couples learning with the pressure concept to coordinate signals along an arterial (Wei et al., 2019a).

These methods have been evaluated mainly in simulation, against conventional and adaptive baselines, under the assumptions of their own test settings. For the present purpose, three properties of the RL family matter. First, learned policies are opaque: the reason for a chosen phase cannot be read from the network weights in a form suitable for a regulator. Second, they are trained for a particular demand distribution, so behaviour under an unfamiliar scenario is not guaranteed by the training procedure. Third, the cited works treat connectivity, power and detector availability as given rather than as design inputs. These observations are the author's reading of the literature rather than findings reported by it. They motivate keeping RL controllers as a comparison point for future work while not relying on them in the first system. The dissertation does not build a deep RL controller; its absence from the experiments is disclosed in the methodology and results chapters and is carried into the future work.

### 2.1.4 Large language models for signal control

The most recent line of work uses large language models (LLMs) as the controller. LLMLight presents the LLM as a traffic signal control agent that receives a textual description of the traffic state and produces a phase decision together with its reasoning (Lai et al., 2023). The attraction for this dissertation is plain: a decision accompanied by language can be inspected, and a language model can in principle take into account contextual information that a queue-based rule cannot represent. The same design also exposes the main difficulty. A language model that issues the phase command itself must respond at the decision frequency of the junction, and a large model reached over a network cannot be assumed to be available at every decision. Latency, cost and dependence on a remote service are properties of this placement of the model, and the cited work does not frame them as constraints of low-connectivity deployments.

This dissertation therefore adopts the idea of LLM-style reasoning about traffic context but not its placement in the control loop. In the proposed design the reasoning model runs infrequently and emits a compact subgoal with a justification, while a lightweight planner on site chooses phase timing. LLMLight is a natural comparison for future work and, as stated in the introduction, it is not among the controllers implemented and evaluated here.

## 2.2 World Models and Model-Predictive Planning

### 2.2.1 Latent world models

A world model is a learned model of how the environment changes in response to actions. When the model operates in a compact latent space rather than on pixels, it can be rolled forward cheaply, which allows an agent to evaluate candidate action sequences without acting. Latent planning from pixels was demonstrated by PlaNet, which learned a latent dynamics model and planned within it (Hafner et al., 2019). Chua et al. (2018) showed that probabilistic dynamics models combined with model-predictive control can reach strong performance with relatively few interactions with the environment, a property that is attractive wherever real data are scarce. MuZero extended the principle to settings in which the model predicts only the quantities needed for planning, rather than reconstructing the observation (Schrittwieser et al., 2020).

DreamerV3 learns a world model and trains behaviour from imagined latent trajectories, and its authors report that a single configuration of hyperparameters was applied across a wide range of control domains (Hafner et al., 2025). TD-MPC2 combines a latent, decoder-free world model with planning at inference time and was presented as a scalable and robust method for continuous control (Hansen et al., 2024). V-JEPA 2 takes a different route: a joint-embedding predictive model trained by self-supervision on video and then adapted with a comparatively small amount of robot interaction data so that it can be used for prediction and planning (Assran et al., 2025). DayDreamer reported that Dreamer-style world models can be trained directly on physical robots, which is evidence that the approach does not depend solely on simulators (Wu et al., 2022). Language can also be placed inside the model: Dynalang learns to predict future observations and text jointly, so that language informs the dynamics (Lin et al., 2024).

For an edge deployment, the relevant question is size and cost. The cited works describe the capabilities of the models; they do not, in general, report on the budget of a roadside computer. The dissertation takes the position, stated as a design choice and not as a literature finding, that a small latent model trained on junction data is the appropriate scale for the fast loop, and that the larger models named above are better treated as references and sources of design ideas than as components for deployment. Following the project's constraint of adopting only open-source, open-weight models and not training foundation models from scratch, DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2 are the candidate families for the world model, while V-JEPA 2 is regarded as a heavier option suited to offline training.

### 2.2.2 Planning with the cross-entropy method

Model-predictive control selects an action by optimising predicted outcomes over a short horizon, executing the first part and then replanning. In learned-model settings the optimiser is commonly a sampling method. The cross-entropy method (CEM) draws candidate action sequences from a parametric distribution, evaluates them, keeps the best fraction, and refits the distribution to that elite set, repeating for a small number of iterations (Rubinstein, 1999; de Boer et al., 2005). It needs no gradients through the model, handles non-differentiable objectives, and its cost is controlled directly by the number of samples and iterations. PlaNet used CEM within a latent model to plan from pixels (Hafner et al., 2019), and the probabilistic-ensemble work of Chua et al. (2018) used sampling-based trajectory optimisation in the same spirit. TD-MPC2 likewise plans at inference time over its latent model (Hansen et al., 2024).

For signal control, three features of CEM are useful. The objective can be written as a weighted sum of queue, delay and pedestrian terms, so that the weights can be changed without retraining the model. The computation budget is predictable, which matters on constrained hardware. Finally, the candidate plans are explicit phase timings, which can be passed through a rule-based check before they are applied. These features are the reason the proposed system exposes cost weights and constraint bounds as the subgoal interface to the planner. They are properties of the method as described in the cited sources, applied here as a design argument.

### 2.2.3 Hierarchical planning with world models

Director is a hierarchical agent in which a manager operating in the latent space of a world model selects goals and a worker is trained to achieve them (Hafner et al., 2022). Its relevance is structural: it shows that a slow component can choose goals for a fast component without issuing the low-level actions itself, which is the arrangement adopted in this dissertation. The difference is that, in the present proposal, the slow component is a language-capable model that reads context outside the sensor stream, and the goal is expressed as a small vector of objective weights and bounds that a human can read.

Other work joins generative models to action generation more tightly. UniPi casts decision-making as text-conditioned video generation followed by an inverse-dynamics step (Du et al., 2023), and SuSIE uses an image-editing diffusion model to propose subgoal images for a low-level policy (Black et al., 2024). RoboDreamer learns compositional world models for imagination (Zhou et al., 2024), and 3D-VLA couples a three-dimensional vision-language-action model with a generative world model (Zhen et al., 2024). These systems show that a generative model of the future can be coupled with action generation. The cited works address manipulation and robotics, and the dissertation does not claim that their results carry over to traffic. A recurring theme in position and survey papers is that perception, reasoning and prediction are developed separately and that a unified model is a long-term aim (Feng et al., 2025; Xu, 2025; Gupta et al., 2024). The present work treats full unification as an open research problem, a view that the authors' earlier manuscript also adopts (Ariyo & Fasina, 2026), and deliberately couples two separate models through a narrow interface.

## 2.3 Vision-Language-Action Models and Chain-of-Thought for Control

### 2.3.1 Vision-language-action models

Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend a pretrained vision-language model so that it outputs actions. RT-2 was an early demonstration that web-scale vision-language knowledge could transfer to robotic control by expressing actions as tokens (Zitkovich et al., 2023). Its weights are not open, so it is cited here as related work and is not a candidate component. The RT-X models and the Open X-Embodiment collection showed the benefit of pooling data from many robot embodiments (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024). Octo is an open generalist policy (Octo Model Team et al., 2024), and OpenVLA is an open-weight VLA that its authors report as outperforming a much larger closed model on the evaluation tasks they used, while also supporting parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Kim et al., 2024). The OpenVLA-OFT recipe was proposed to improve fine-tuning speed and task success (Kim et al., 2025), and π0 pairs a vision-language backbone with a flow-matching action model (Black et al., 2025). GR00T N1 is released as an open foundation model for humanoid robots (NVIDIA et al., 2025). Surveys document the breadth of this literature (Sapkota et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), and the wider background of foundation models, including their risks, is set out by Bommasani et al. (2021). Earlier multimodal and generalist systems such as CLIP, Flamingo, PaLM-E, Gato and VIMA supply the lineage on which these models rest (Radford et al., 2021; Alayrac et al., 2022; Driess et al., 2023; Reed et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023).

Two observations follow for this dissertation. The VLA literature is almost entirely concerned with robot manipulation and humanoid control, so its models are trained on arm and hand action spaces rather than on signal phases. Adopting a VLA for traffic therefore cannot mean using a manipulation checkpoint as a controller. The second observation is that most of these models are large. OpenVLA, for example, is built on a multi-billion-parameter language backbone, and the cited works report their latency on server-class graphics processors rather than on roadside hardware. For SSA deployment, this is a reason to place the VLA-style model in a slow loop that runs rarely, and not in the fast loop.

### 2.3.2 Chain-of-thought reasoning for control

Embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT) trains a VLA to produce intermediate reasoning steps before it outputs an action, including a plan, subtasks, motion descriptions and the locations of relevant objects, and its authors report improved generalisation of OpenVLA-style policies on the tasks they studied (Zawalski et al., 2024). The same work acknowledges the cost of producing the extra tokens at inference time. InstructVLA addresses a related tension: instruction tuning for manipulation can erode the multimodal reasoning of the underlying model, and the proposed training recipe is intended to keep understanding and action together (Yang et al., 2025). Both works matter to the dissertation for the same reason: they show that a written reasoning trace can be produced alongside control, which supports the explainability objective, and that the trace has a cost in time.

The present design uses this finding differently from the cited works. In ECoT the reasoning is generated before every low-level action, so its latency is paid at the control rate. In the proposed system the reasoning is generated on the slow timescale, where seconds or tens of seconds are acceptable, and its output is a justification plus a subgoal. The trace is then stored for audit. The reliability of reasoning traces produced by small models is not guaranteed by these works, and the dissertation reports its own smoke test of a small open model in the results chapter rather than assuming reliability.

### 2.3.3 Hierarchical and dual-system designs

Several systems split the problem into a slow, deliberative component and a fast, reactive one. Hi Robot places a high-level vision-language model above a low-level VLA, with the upper model producing language instructions and responding to feedback from the user, while the lower model executes them (Shi et al., 2025). Helix, a technical report from a commercial developer, describes a slow system for scene understanding and language and a fast system for continuous control within one humanoid platform (Figure AI, 2025). GR00T N1 is likewise described with two cooperating modules (NVIDIA et al., 2025). Director, discussed above, supplies the hierarchical-planning counterpart on the world-model side (Hafner et al., 2022).

These designs establish that the slow and fast split is a recognised pattern, and the dissertation does not claim novelty for the split itself. What the cited works do not provide, as far as the verified literature reviewed here shows, is a version designed around the constraints of SSA infrastructure, with an explicit fallback when the slow component is unreachable, a rule-based safety layer between planner and actuator, and an audit trail of reasoning intended for a transport regulator. The authors' earlier manuscript set out the low-resource rationale for the two-timescale pattern as a design proposal (Ariyo & Fasina, 2026); this dissertation instantiates a traffic-control version and evaluates part of it in simulation.

### 2.3.4 Open vision-language models for the slow loop

The slow loop requires a model that reads images and text. Qwen2.5-VL is an open-weight vision-language model family released in several sizes (Bai et al., 2025). The availability of a small variant is relevant because it makes an on-site or cluster-level slow loop conceivable on modest hardware, while a larger variant could be run in the cloud when connectivity allows. This size ladder is the basis of the degradation tiers described in the methodology chapter. The statement that a given variant is adequate for traffic reasoning is not supported by the cited technical report, which evaluates general benchmarks. Whether a small model reasons reliably about a junction is an empirical question that the dissertation examines only through a limited smoke test.

### 2.3.5 Safety of embodied language-model agents

If a language-capable model is placed anywhere near actuation, its failure modes matter. SafeAgentBench is a benchmark for safe task planning by embodied LLM agents and was introduced because such agents can produce unsafe plans (Yin et al., 2024). SafeEmbodAI proposes a safety framework for mobile robots in embodied AI systems (Zhang et al., 2024). Both works are preprints and concern settings other than traffic, but they are consistent with a design principle adopted here: learned components should not be the last line of defence. In the proposed system, a rule-based shield enforces minimum green, amber, all-red clearance and pedestrian crossing time between the planner and the signal controller, and nothing learned can bypass it. This is a conventional engineering safeguard for signals, and the literature on embodied agents reinforces the case for it.

## 2.4 Explainability and the Regulatory Setting

Explainability in traffic control has two audiences. Operators need to understand why a signal behaved as it did on a given day, and a public authority needs to be able to review whether the behaviour was consistent with its rules. The project context from which this dissertation derives names the Lagos State Traffic Management Authority (LASTMA) and the Ministry of Transportation as the audit stakeholders for the system's decisions, and the design therefore keeps a timestamped record of each decision together with the reasoning trace and the conditions that triggered it. These roles are stated here as the author's setting and design assumption. This chapter does not report the content of any regulation or audit procedure of either body, and the design should be reviewed with them before any claim of regulatory suitability is made.

The literature reviewed above offers three relevant observations. Learned policies that output only an action, as is typical in RL signal control, give no record of their reasoning (Wei et al., 2019c). Max-pressure is transparent in the sense that its rule can be stated in one sentence (Varaiya, 2013), but it cannot explain a context-dependent departure from that rule. Language-based controllers and chain-of-thought VLAs produce readable traces (Lai et al., 2023; Zawalski et al., 2024), but a readable trace is not necessarily a faithful account of the computation. The dissertation therefore treats a trace as a record for audit and human review, not as proof of correct reasoning, and pairs it with the shield so that safety does not depend on the trace being right.

Counterfactual analysis is a further route to explanation that a world model makes available in principle. Because the model can be rolled forward under alternative actions, it can be asked what would have happened had a different phase plan been chosen. The cited world-model literature uses imagination for planning and not for explanation (Hafner et al., 2025; Hansen et al., 2024), so the use of rollouts for what-if explanation is a proposal of this work and is described as such in the framework discussion. The explainability layer of the eight-layer deployment framework is intended to hold this material. The framework itself originates with Dr. Fasina, who proposed the eight layers in the class session of 27 July 2026, and the way the author's refinements treat that layer is described in the methodology chapter.

## 2.5 Infrastructure Constraints in Sub-Saharan Africa

### 2.5.1 Connectivity, power and compute

Most of the work reviewed in the preceding sections assumes dependable connectivity, ample compute and stable power. The available regional statistics indicate that these assumptions are weak across much of SSA. The GSMA reported mobile internet penetration in Sub-Saharan Africa of 27% by the end of 2023 (GSMA, 2024a), and its global connectivity report (GSMA, 2024b) provides the worldwide comparison. On electricity, the World Bank indicator records that about 53.3% of the SSA population had access in 2023, against about 91.6% worldwide (World Bank, 2026), and the multi-agency energy progress report states that 565 million people in the region lacked electricity in that year, about 85% of the global total of people without access (International Energy Agency et al., 2025; United Nations Statistics Division, 2025). The Africa Data Centres Association places the continent's share of global data-centre capacity at 0.6% (Africa Data Centres Association, 2026). A recent analysis of embodied AI for science in the Global South similarly argues that infrastructure comes first, and that methods must be shaped around the available compute, connectivity and energy (Liu et al., 2026).

These figures describe populations and regions, not individual junctions. A signalised junction in a major city may have a better power supply and mobile coverage than the regional average, and the statistics should be read as evidence that the assumptions are not safe to make, not as measurements of any particular site. This is why the dissertation treats connectivity, power and compute as design inputs and builds the control loop so that its safety-critical part runs on site.

### 2.5.2 Camera-based traffic systems in African cities

Cities in the region are investing in camera-based and centrally coordinated signal systems. Public reports describe the selection of a vendor for an intelligent traffic system in Lagos (Olomu, 2025), a phased deployment of AI-based traffic systems by the Lagos Metropolitan Area Transport Authority (ITEdgeNews, 2026), and commentary on whether AI could help with congestion in Lagos (Adeyemo, 2026). In Kenya, the Kenya Urban Roads Authority (2023) has published on intelligent transport systems, and the press has reported on Nairobi programmes and tenders for AI-enabled signals (Kinyanjui, 2024; Mumbi, 2026; Nairobi Law Monthly, 2026). These sources are news and agency reports. They are cited as evidence that interest in such systems exists and that procurement activity is under way, not as technical evaluations, and this chapter does not rely on them for performance claims. They also do not describe the control algorithms used, so no comparison with the proposed design is drawn from them.

### 2.5.3 Mixed traffic and informal transit

The project context identifies a traffic mix that includes okadas, kekes, danfo, matatus and boda-boda, with motorcycle lane-splitting among the behaviours of interest. The verified reference set used for this dissertation does not include a source that characterises this mix quantitatively for the target corridor, and this chapter makes no quantitative claim about it. The implication for the literature review is nevertheless clear. The signal-control studies cited above are, to the extent that their descriptions are available to this review, framed around conventional vehicles, and the proposed work accordingly treats the representation of mixed traffic in simulation as a limitation to be disclosed, not as a solved problem.

## 2.7 Synthesis and Research Gap

Four threads emerge from the review. Signal control has moved from fixed rules to learned and language-based controllers, but the more capable the controller, the less likely it is to be both explainable and independent of remote services. Latent world models with sampling-based planning provide a local, inexpensive, objective-driven way to choose actions, with weights that can be changed without retraining. VLA and chain-of-thought work shows that reasoning traces can accompany control, at a latency cost that suits a slower timescale. Safety work on embodied language agents is consistent with the use of a rule-based layer that nothing learned can override.

Against this background the following gap is identified. The literature reviewed here treats these threads separately. Signal-control studies address junctions but do not take language-based context or infrastructure scarcity as design inputs. VLA and world-model studies address reasoning and prediction but are concerned with manipulation and humanoid robots, and assume resources that are not safe to assume in SSA. Deployment reports from African cities show interest but do not describe algorithms. No source in the verified set combines a slow, language-capable context reader, a fast local world-model planner, a rule-based safety shield, a timestamped reasoning log for audit and an explicit degradation path for loss of connectivity within a single junction or corridor design. This statement is limited to the sources reviewed in this chapter, and it is not a claim that no such work exists.

The dissertation responds with a proposal and a limited test. The proposal is a coupled two-timescale design, with the VLAM and the latent world model kept as separate models linked only by a compact subgoal vector, since full unification remains an open research problem and coupling is a deliberate choice under the compute and connectivity limits described above. The test is a simulation study at a single junction using SUMO (Alvarez Lopez et al., 2018), which compares the fast-loop components against fixed-time, count-plus-timer and max-pressure baselines, with the slow loop implemented but not yet evaluated. The remaining chapters set out the design, report what was and was not evaluated, and identify the missing baselines, deep RL and LLMLight, as future work.
