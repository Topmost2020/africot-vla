# Chapter 2: Literature Review

This chapter reviews the work on which the proposed traffic-control system builds: traffic signal control, from fixed-time plans to control by large language models; latent world models and planning; vision-language-action models and chain-of-thought reasoning; explainability and the regulatory setting; and the infrastructure constraints of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). It closes with a synthesis that states the gap the dissertation addresses. Where the literature does not support a claim, the claim is omitted or is stated as the author's own reasoning.

## 2.1 Traffic Signal Control

### 2.1.1 Fixed-time and actuated control

The simplest form of signal control repeats a pre-computed cycle whose phase durations are set from historical demand. Fixed-time plans need no sensing and little computation, which helps to explain their persistence in cities with limited detector coverage. Their weakness is that the plan cannot respond to demand that departs from its assumptions, such as a market day, a stalled vehicle or a burst of motorcycle traffic. Actuated control relaxes this by extending or terminating a phase according to detector calls, within bounds set by the engineer. Surveys treat these two families as the conventional baseline against which adaptive and learning-based methods are compared (Wei et al., 2019c). Both continue to operate when communication fails, because nothing outside the junction is required. The proposed system retains a fixed-time plan for exactly this reason, as the last tier of its degradation ladder.

### 2.1.2 Max-pressure and queue-based adaptive control

Max-pressure control is a decentralised policy for a network of signalised intersections that chooses, at each decision point, the phase that relieves the greatest pressure, where pressure is the difference between the queue upstream of a movement and the queues on the downstream links it feeds (Varaiya, 2013). The method needs only local queue information, requires no knowledge of demand, and is accompanied by an analytical stability result under the model assumptions of the original work. Because it is inexpensive and well understood, it is a standard non-learning adaptive baseline, and it is the reference against which the controllers in this dissertation are compared. Its limits are visible from its definition: it optimises a queue-difference quantity rather than travel time or the priority of particular road users, and it cannot take into account context such as a market, an event or an incident.

Wei et al. (2019a) used the pressure concept to design the state and reward of a reinforcement-learning agent, which links the analytic and learned traditions. The slow-to-fast interface in the methodology chapter likewise uses weights on queue-related terms, not phase commands.

### 2.1.3 Reinforcement-learning methods

Reinforcement learning (RL) treats signal control as a sequential decision problem in which an agent observes a traffic state, selects a phase or duration, and receives a reward derived from queues, delay or throughput. Wei et al. (2019c) survey this literature by state representation, action definition and reward design. CoLight introduced graph attention so that an intersection can weigh the influence of its neighbours (Wei et al., 2019b), and Chu et al. (2020) developed a multi-agent scheme intended for large-scale networks. PressLight couples learning with the pressure concept to coordinate signals along an arterial (Wei et al., 2019a).

Three properties matter here. First, learned policies are opaque: the reason for a chosen phase cannot be read from the weights in a form suitable for a regulator. Second, they are trained for a particular demand distribution, so behaviour under an unfamiliar scenario is not guaranteed. Third, the cited works treat connectivity, power and detector availability as given rather than as design inputs. These observations are the author's reading of the literature, not findings reported by it. The dissertation does not build a deep RL controller; its absence is disclosed in the methodology and results chapters and carried into the future work.

### 2.1.4 Large language models for signal control

The most recent line of work uses large language models (LLMs) as the controller. LLMLight presents the LLM as a signal control agent that receives a textual description of the traffic state and produces a phase decision together with its reasoning (Lai et al., 2023). A decision accompanied by language can be inspected, but this design has a difficulty. A model that issues the phase command itself must respond at the decision frequency of the junction, and a large model reached over a network cannot be assumed to be available at every decision. Latency, cost and dependence on a remote service are properties of this placement, and the cited work does not frame them as constraints of low-connectivity deployments.

This dissertation therefore adopts LLM-style reasoning about traffic context but not its placement in the control loop. The reasoning model runs infrequently and emits a compact subgoal with a justification, while a lightweight planner on site chooses phase timing. LLMLight is a natural comparison for future work and, as stated in the introduction, is not among the controllers evaluated here.

## 2.2 World Models and Model-Predictive Planning

### 2.2.1 Latent world models

A world model is a learned model of how the environment changes in response to actions. When it operates in a compact latent space rather than on pixels, it can be rolled forward cheaply, allowing an agent to evaluate candidate action sequences without acting. PlaNet learned a latent dynamics model and planned within it from pixels (Hafner et al., 2019). Chua et al. (2018) showed that probabilistic dynamics models combined with model-predictive control can perform well with relatively few interactions, which is attractive wherever real data are scarce. MuZero extended the principle to models that predict only the quantities needed for planning (Schrittwieser et al., 2020).

DreamerV3 learns a world model and trains behaviour from imagined latent trajectories, and its authors report that a single hyperparameter configuration was applied across a wide range of control domains (Hafner et al., 2025). TD-MPC2 combines a latent, decoder-free world model with planning at inference time (Hansen et al., 2024). V-JEPA 2 is a joint-embedding predictive model trained by self-supervision on video and adapted with a comparatively small amount of robot interaction data (Assran et al., 2025). DayDreamer reported that Dreamer-style models can be trained directly on physical robots (Wu et al., 2022), and Dynalang learns to predict future observations and text jointly (Lin et al., 2024).

For an edge deployment, size and cost matter, and the cited works do not, in general, report the budget of a roadside computer. The dissertation takes the position, as a design choice and not a literature finding, that a small latent model trained on junction data is the appropriate scale for the fast loop, and that the larger models named above are better treated as references than as deployment components. Under the open-weight constraint, DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2 are the candidate families for the world model, while V-JEPA 2 is regarded as a heavier option suited to offline training.

### 2.2.2 Planning with the cross-entropy method

Model-predictive control selects an action by optimising predicted outcomes over a short horizon, executing the first part and then replanning. In learned-model settings the optimiser is commonly a sampling method. The cross-entropy method (CEM) draws candidate action sequences from a parametric distribution, evaluates them, keeps the best fraction, and refits the distribution to that elite set, repeating for a small number of iterations (Rubinstein, 1999; de Boer et al., 2005). It needs no gradients through the model, handles non-differentiable objectives, and its cost is controlled directly by the number of samples and iterations. PlaNet used CEM within a latent model (Hafner et al., 2019), Chua et al. (2018) used sampling-based trajectory optimisation in the same spirit, and TD-MPC2 likewise plans at inference time (Hansen et al., 2024).

For signal control, three features of CEM are useful. The objective can be a weighted sum of queue, delay and pedestrian terms, so the weights can be changed without retraining the model. The computation budget is predictable, which matters on constrained hardware. The candidate plans are explicit phase timings, which can be passed through a rule-based check before they are applied. These features are the reason the proposed system exposes cost weights and constraint bounds as the subgoal interface to the planner.

### 2.2.3 Hierarchical planning with world models

Director is a hierarchical agent in which a manager operating in the latent space of a world model selects goals and a worker is trained to achieve them (Hafner et al., 2022). Its relevance is structural: a slow component can choose goals for a fast component without issuing the low-level actions itself, which is the arrangement adopted here. Here the slow component is a language-capable model that reads context outside the sensor stream.

Other work joins generative models to action generation more tightly. UniPi casts decision-making as text-conditioned video generation followed by an inverse-dynamics step (Du et al., 2023), SuSIE uses an image-editing diffusion model to propose subgoal images (Black et al., 2024), RoboDreamer learns compositional world models for imagination (Zhou et al., 2024), and 3D-VLA couples a three-dimensional VLA model with a generative world model (Zhen et al., 2024). These works address robotics, and their results are not assumed to carry over to traffic. A recurring theme in position and survey papers is that perception, reasoning and prediction are developed separately and that a unified model is a long-term aim (Feng et al., 2025; Xu, 2025; Gupta et al., 2024). The present work treats full unification as an open research problem, a view that the authors' earlier manuscript also adopts (Ariyo & Fasina, 2026), and deliberately couples two separate models through a narrow interface.

## 2.3 Vision-Language-Action Models and Chain-of-Thought for Control

### 2.3.1 Vision-language-action models

Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend a pretrained vision-language model so that it outputs actions. RT-2 showed that web-scale vision-language knowledge could transfer to robotic control by expressing actions as tokens (Zitkovich et al., 2023); its weights are not open, so it is cited as related work and is not a candidate component. The Open X-Embodiment collection showed the benefit of pooling data from many robot embodiments (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024). Octo is an open generalist policy (Octo Model Team et al., 2024), and OpenVLA is an open-weight VLA that its authors report as outperforming a much larger closed model on their evaluation tasks, while supporting parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Kim et al., 2024). OpenVLA-OFT was proposed to improve fine-tuning speed and task success (Kim et al., 2025), π0 pairs a vision-language backbone with a flow-matching action model (Black et al., 2025), and GR00T N1 is an open foundation model for humanoid robots (NVIDIA et al., 2025). Surveys document the breadth of this literature (Sapkota et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), the wider background of foundation models is set out by Bommasani et al. (2021), and CLIP, Flamingo, PaLM-E, Gato and VIMA supply the lineage on which these models rest (Radford et al., 2021; Alayrac et al., 2022; Driess et al., 2023; Reed et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023).

Two observations follow. The VLA literature is almost entirely concerned with manipulation and humanoid control, so its models are trained on arm and hand action spaces rather than signal phases, and adopting a VLA for traffic cannot mean using a manipulation checkpoint as a controller. Most of these models are also large and are reported on server-class processors rather than roadside hardware. For SSA deployment, this is a reason to place the VLA-style model in a slow loop that runs rarely, and not in the fast loop.

### 2.3.2 Chain-of-thought reasoning for control

Embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT) trains a VLA to produce intermediate reasoning steps before it outputs an action, including a plan, subtasks, motion descriptions and object locations, and its authors report improved generalisation of OpenVLA-style policies on the tasks they studied (Zawalski et al., 2024). InstructVLA addresses a related tension: instruction tuning for manipulation can erode the multimodal reasoning of the underlying model, and its training recipe is intended to keep understanding and action together (Yang et al., 2025). Both show that a written trace can accompany control, which supports explainability, at a cost in time.

In ECoT the reasoning precedes every low-level action, so its latency is paid at the control rate. In the proposed system the reasoning is generated on the slow timescale, where seconds or tens of seconds are acceptable, and its output is a justification plus a subgoal, stored for audit. These works do not guarantee the reliability of traces produced by small models, and the dissertation reports its own smoke test of a small open model in the results chapter rather than assuming reliability.

### 2.3.3 Hierarchical and dual-system designs

Several systems split the problem into a slow, deliberative component and a fast, reactive one. Hi Robot places a high-level vision-language model above a low-level VLA, with the upper model producing language instructions and responding to user feedback (Shi et al., 2025). Helix, a technical report from a commercial developer, describes a slow system for scene understanding and a fast system for continuous control within one humanoid platform (Figure AI, 2025). GR00T N1 is likewise described with two cooperating modules (NVIDIA et al., 2025), and Director supplies the counterpart on the world-model side (Hafner et al., 2022).

These designs establish that the slow and fast split is a recognised pattern, and the dissertation does not claim novelty for the split itself. What the cited works do not provide, as far as the verified literature reviewed here shows, is a version designed around SSA infrastructure constraints, with an explicit fallback when the slow component is unreachable, a rule-based safety layer between planner and actuator, and an audit trail of reasoning intended for a transport regulator. The authors' earlier manuscript set out the low-resource rationale for the two-timescale pattern as a design proposal (Ariyo & Fasina, 2026).

### 2.3.4 Open vision-language models for the slow loop

The slow loop requires a model that reads images and text. Qwen2.5-VL is an open-weight vision-language model family released in several sizes (Bai et al., 2025). A small variant makes an on-site or cluster-level slow loop conceivable on modest hardware, while a larger variant could be hosted in the cloud when connectivity allows, which is the basis of the degradation tiers described in the methodology chapter. The cited technical report evaluates general benchmarks and does not support the statement that a given variant is adequate for traffic reasoning. Whether a small model reasons reliably about a junction is an empirical question that the dissertation examines only through a limited smoke test.

A cloud-hosted slow loop with an edge fallback is the placement this dissertation intends. The reasoning model, the data store and the audit log are intended to be hosted on the cloud, in line with the supervisor's cloud-based framework, while the fast loop and the safety shield remain on site so that the junction keeps operating when the link drops. The literature reviewed here does not evaluate such a split for signal control, and the placement is therefore presented as a design proposal.

### 2.3.5 Safety of embodied language-model agents

If a language-capable model is placed anywhere near actuation, its failure modes matter. SafeAgentBench is a benchmark for safe task planning by embodied LLM agents, introduced because such agents can produce unsafe plans (Yin et al., 2024), and SafeEmbodAI proposes a safety framework for mobile robots in embodied AI systems (Zhang et al., 2024). Both are preprints concerning settings other than traffic, but they are consistent with a principle adopted here: learned components should not be the last line of defence. A rule-based shield enforces minimum green, amber, all-red clearance and pedestrian crossing time between the planner and the signal controller, and nothing learned can bypass it.

## 2.4 Explainability and the Regulatory Setting

Explainability in traffic control serves operators and a public authority that reviews whether behaviour was consistent with its rules. The project context names the Lagos State Traffic Management Authority (LASTMA) and the Ministry of Transportation as the audit stakeholders, and the design therefore keeps a timestamped record of each decision together with the reasoning trace and the triggering conditions. This chapter does not report the content of any regulation or audit procedure of either body, and the design should be reviewed with them before any claim of regulatory suitability is made.

The literature offers three relevant observations. Learned policies that output only an action, as is typical in RL signal control, give no record of their reasoning (Wei et al., 2019c). Max-pressure is transparent in the sense that its rule can be stated in one sentence (Varaiya, 2013), but it cannot explain a context-dependent departure from that rule. Language-based controllers and chain-of-thought VLAs produce readable traces (Lai et al., 2023; Zawalski et al., 2024), but a readable trace is not necessarily a faithful account of the computation. The dissertation therefore treats a trace as a record for audit and human review, not as proof of correct reasoning, and pairs it with the shield so that safety does not depend on the trace being right.

Counterfactual analysis is a further route to explanation that a world model makes available in principle: because the model can be rolled forward under alternative actions, it can be asked what would have happened had a different phase plan been chosen. The cited world-model literature uses imagination for planning and not for explanation (Hafner et al., 2025; Hansen et al., 2024), so the use of rollouts for what-if explanation is a proposal of this work. The framework originates with Dr. Fasina, who proposed the eight layers in the class session of 27 July 2026, and the author's refinements are described in the methodology chapter.

## 2.5 Infrastructure Constraints in Sub-Saharan Africa

### 2.5.1 Connectivity, power and compute

Most of the work reviewed above assumes dependable connectivity, ample compute and stable power. The available regional statistics indicate that these assumptions are weak across much of SSA. The GSMA reported mobile internet penetration in Sub-Saharan Africa of 27% by the end of 2023 (GSMA, 2024a), and its global report (GSMA, 2024b) provides the worldwide comparison. On electricity, the World Bank indicator records that about 53.3% of the SSA population had access in 2023, against about 91.6% worldwide (World Bank, 2026), and the multi-agency energy progress report states that 565 million people in the region lacked electricity in that year, about 85% of the global total (International Energy Agency et al., 2025; United Nations Statistics Division, 2025). The Africa Data Centres Association places the continent's share of global data-centre capacity at 0.6% (Africa Data Centres Association, 2026). A recent analysis of embodied AI for science in the Global South argues that methods must be shaped around the available compute, connectivity and energy (Liu et al., 2026).

These figures describe populations and regions, not individual junctions. A junction in a major city may have better power and coverage than the regional average, so the statistics show only that the assumptions are unsafe, not the state of any site. This is why the dissertation treats connectivity, power and compute as design inputs and builds the control loop so that its safety-critical part runs on site.

### 2.5.2 Camera-based traffic systems in African cities

Cities in the region are investing in camera-based and centrally coordinated signal systems. Public reports describe the selection of a vendor for an intelligent traffic system in Lagos (Olomu, 2025), a phased deployment of AI-based traffic systems by the Lagos Metropolitan Area Transport Authority (ITEdgeNews, 2026), and commentary on whether AI could help with congestion in Lagos (Adeyemo, 2026). In Kenya, the Kenya Urban Roads Authority (2023) has published on intelligent transport systems, and the press has reported on Nairobi programmes and tenders for AI-enabled signals (Kinyanjui, 2024; Mumbi, 2026; Nairobi Law Monthly, 2026). These sources are news and agency reports, cited only as evidence that interest and procurement activity exist. They do not describe the control algorithms used.

### 2.5.3 Mixed traffic and informal transit

The project context identifies a traffic mix that includes okadas, kekes, danfo, matatus and boda-boda, with motorcycle lane-splitting among the behaviours of interest. The verified reference set does not include a source that characterises this mix quantitatively for the target corridor, and this chapter makes no quantitative claim about it. The studies cited above appear to be framed around conventional vehicles, so mixed traffic in simulation is treated as a disclosed limitation.

## 2.7 Synthesis and Research Gap

Four threads emerge from the review. Signal control has moved from fixed rules to learned and language-based controllers, but the more capable the controller, the less likely it is to be both explainable and independent of remote services. Latent world models with sampling-based planning provide a local, inexpensive, objective-driven way to choose actions, with weights that can be changed without retraining. VLA and chain-of-thought work shows that reasoning traces can accompany control, at a latency cost that suits a slower timescale. Safety work on embodied language agents is consistent with a rule-based layer that nothing learned can override.

Against this background the following gap is identified. The literature treats these threads separately. Signal-control studies address junctions but do not take language-based context or infrastructure scarcity as design inputs. VLA and world-model studies address reasoning and prediction but concern manipulation and humanoid robots, and assume resources that are not safe to assume in SSA. No source in the verified set combines a slow, language-capable context reader, a fast local world-model planner, a rule-based safety shield, a timestamped reasoning log for audit and an explicit degradation path for loss of connectivity within a single junction or corridor design. This statement is limited to the sources reviewed here, and it is not a claim that no such work exists.

The dissertation responds with a proposal and a limited test. The proposal is a coupled two-timescale design, with the VLAM and the latent world model kept as separate models linked only by a compact subgoal vector, since full unification remains an open research problem and coupling is a deliberate choice under the compute and connectivity limits described above. The test is a simulation study at a single junction using SUMO (Alvarez Lopez et al., 2018), comparing the fast-loop components against fixed-time, count-plus-timer and max-pressure baselines, with the slow loop implemented but not yet evaluated. The missing baselines, deep RL and LLMLight, are identified as future work.
